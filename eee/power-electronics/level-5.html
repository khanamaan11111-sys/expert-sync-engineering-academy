DC–AC Inverters

Understand how inverters produce alternating output from a DC source, and learn the essentials of single-phase bridges, three-phase bridges, pulse-width modulation and harmonics. Estimated study time: 50–65 minutes.

Learning objectives

  • Explain the function of a voltage-source inverter.
  • Recognize half-bridge, full-bridge and three-phase inverter configurations.
  • Describe switching sequences, PWM and the role of dead time.
  • Calculate basic output frequency and modulation-index examples.
  • Identify harmonic, filtering and electrical-safety considerations.

1. What is an inverter?

A DC–AC inverter uses semiconductor switches to create an alternating output waveform from a DC supply. Inverters are found in solar power systems, uninterruptible power supplies, variable-frequency motor drives and other applications. A raw switching waveform is not necessarily sinusoidal; modulation and suitable filtering or load characteristics shape the usable output.

Single-phase half-bridge

Two controlled switches and a split DC link can produce positive and negative output levels relative to the DC midpoint. Proper gate timing is essential.

Single-phase full-bridge (H-bridge)

Four controlled switches can reverse the polarity applied to the load. Opposite diagonal switch pairs create positive and negative load voltages.

Three-phase bridge

Three switching legs, typically six controlled devices, generate three phase-related outputs for applications such as AC motor drives.

2. Switching and output frequency

In a simple ideal full-bridge square-wave inverter, the load alternates between +VDC and −VDC. If one complete positive-and-negative cycle lasts T seconds, its fundamental output frequency is:

fout = 1 / T

Example: for a complete cycle of 20 ms (0.020 s), the fundamental frequency is 50 Hz. The switching frequency used in PWM can be much higher than the desired fundamental output frequency.

Never command both devices in the same bridge leg on simultaneously: that can short the DC bus, a fault called shoot-through.

3. Pulse-width modulation (PWM)

Sinusoidal PWM (SPWM) compares a low-frequency sinusoidal reference with a higher-frequency triangular carrier to produce switching pulses. The pulse pattern controls the fundamental output while shifting significant switching harmonics toward carrier-related frequencies.

ma = Vreference,peak / Vcarrier,peak

This is the amplitude modulation index under a consistent comparator convention. In the ideal linear SPWM region (commonly ma ≤ 1), increasing ma increases the fundamental output amplitude. Overmodulation changes the relationship and waveform.

mf = fcarrier / freference

Example: a 10 kHz carrier and a 50 Hz reference give a frequency modulation ratio of 200.

4. Worked examples

Example A: output frequency

A waveform repeats every 0.01 s. The fundamental repetition frequency is 1/0.01 = 100 Hz.

Example B: amplitude modulation

Reference peak = 0.8 V and carrier peak = 1 V: ma = 0.8, within the ideal linear SPWM region.

Example C: carrier ratio

Carrier = 12 kHz and reference = 60 Hz: mf = 12,000/60 = 200.

5. Harmonics, filtering and dead time

Harmonics are frequency components at integer multiples of a fundamental frequency. A square-wave inverter has substantial harmonic content; PWM changes the spectral distribution but does not eliminate harmonics. An appropriate output filter can attenuate unwanted high-frequency components for suitable applications.

Dead time is a short interval inserted between turning off one device and turning on its complementary device in a bridge leg. It helps prevent shoot-through, though it also introduces output distortion that control systems may need to compensate.

Real inverter design also considers DC-link capacitor ratings, device voltage/current stresses, switching losses, thermal management, electromagnetic interference, isolation and protection.

6. Simulation exercise

In a suitable simulator, model a low-voltage idealized single-phase H-bridge supplied from a 24 V DC source and connected to a resistive load. Use complementary diagonal switching with safe non-overlap. Observe the alternating load voltage. Then study a simulator-provided SPWM inverter example, comparing the fundamental reference frequency and the switching carrier frequency.

Record the intended waveform, actual waveform, period and frequency. Simulation is educational; a real hardware implementation requires validated gate drives, isolation and protection.

7. Safety and practice

Inverter DC links can contain hazardous energy even after disconnection. Do not build mains-connected or high-power inverter hardware without qualified supervision, rated test equipment and an appropriate safety procedure. Never probe an energized bridge with improperly grounded instruments.
