Power Semiconductor Devices

Understand how diodes, MOSFETs and IGBTs operate in practical power converters. Estimated study time: 45–60 minutes.

Learning objectives

  • Explain the function of power diodes, MOSFETs and IGBTs.
  • Distinguish conduction losses from switching losses.
  • Recognize important device ratings and gate-driver requirements.
  • Interpret simplified converter device-selection examples.

1. Why semiconductor switches matter

Power electronics controls energy conversion using devices that block or conduct current. Switching rapidly between these states, together with inductors, capacitors and control circuits, enables efficient conversion. Real devices have nonzero losses and must be selected for their electrical and thermal limits.

Power diode

Two-terminal device. It conducts when appropriately forward-biased and blocks reverse voltage within its rating. Often used for rectification and freewheeling paths. Reverse recovery matters in many high-frequency circuits.

Power MOSFET

Voltage-controlled gate device widely used in high-frequency switching. Its on-state loss can be approximated by I² × RDS(on) when fully enhanced. Gate charge and voltage rating affect selection.

IGBT

Insulated-gate bipolar transistor: voltage-controlled gate with bipolar conduction characteristics. Often used in higher-voltage/high-power applications. On-state voltage drop and switching tail current contribute to loss.

2. Comparison and key specifications

Diode: no control gate; check forward current, reverse blocking voltage, forward drop and reverse recovery.

MOSFET: check VDS, drain current, RDS(on) at actual gate voltage and temperature, total gate charge, switching behavior and safe operating area.

IGBT: check VCES, collector current, VCE(sat), switching energy, gate-drive requirements and thermal ratings.

Ratings are not interchangeable. Use the manufacturer's datasheet and account for voltage spikes, current transients, temperature and appropriate design margins.

3. Conduction and switching losses

PMOSFET,cond ≈ IRMS² × RDS(on)

Illustrative resistive-channel approximation for a fully enhanced MOSFET. For example, 5 A RMS through a 0.04 Ω on-resistance gives 1 W during continuous conduction; apply duty fraction and temperature-dependent resistance where relevant.

Pdiode ≈ VF × Iavg

A simplified constant-forward-drop model. For a 0.8 V drop at 2 A average conduction current, loss is approximately 1.6 W.

Pswitch ≈ ½ × V × I × (tr + tf) × fs

First-order estimate for hard switching under simplified linear overlap assumptions. Real losses also depend on device capacitance, diode recovery, gate drive and circuit conditions; use datasheet switching-energy curves for detailed design.

4. Gate driving and protection

A gate driver supplies the required gate voltage and charge/discharge current. A MOSFET is not guaranteed fully on merely because its threshold voltage has been reached. Use datasheet-specified drive conditions. IGBTs likewise require appropriate gate-drive design.

Practical systems may need isolation, undervoltage lockout, dead time in bridge circuits, overcurrent protection, snubbers or clamps, and thermal management. Incorrect dead time can cause shoot-through in a bridge.

5. Worked examples

Example A: MOSFET conduction

At 4 A RMS and RDS(on) = 0.05 Ω, continuous-conduction estimate: 4² × 0.05 = 0.8 W.

Example B: diode loss

At VF = 0.9 V and Iavg = 3 A, estimate: 0.9 × 3 = 2.7 W.

Example C: switching estimate

For 100 V, 2 A, tr + tf = 100 ns, fs = 20 kHz: 0.2 W under the simplified overlap model.

6. Safety and practice

Do not build or test mains-connected or high-energy converters without qualified supervision, appropriate isolation, rated equipment and a documented safety procedure. DC-link capacitors can retain hazardous charge after power-off. Study datasheets and simulations first.
