Rectifiers & AC–DC Conversion

Learn how rectifier circuits convert alternating voltage into unidirectional output and how filtering improves DC quality. Estimated study time: 45–60 minutes.

Learning objectives

  • Differentiate half-wave, full-wave center-tapped and bridge rectifiers.
  • Understand uncontrolled diode rectifiers versus phase-controlled rectifiers.
  • Calculate idealized average rectified voltage for sinusoidal inputs.
  • Explain ripple, filter capacitors and practical component ratings.

1. What is rectification?

A rectifier converts AC into an output whose polarity is predominantly one direction. An unfiltered rectifier output is pulsating DC, not perfectly constant DC. Practical AC–DC supplies may also require isolation, filtering, regulation and protection.

Half-wave rectifier

One diode passes one half-cycle of a sinusoidal input and blocks the other. Simple, but with substantial ripple and relatively poor utilization of the source.

Full-wave center-tapped

Two diodes with a suitable center-tapped transformer use alternate half-cycles to produce the same output polarity across the load.

Full-wave bridge

Four diodes form a bridge; two conduct during each half-cycle. A center-tapped transformer is not required. The load sees both halves with the same polarity.

2. Ideal rectifier equations

For a sinusoidal source v(t) = Vm sin(ωt), where Vm is the peak voltage, and an ideal resistive load with no smoothing capacitor:

Vavg,half = Vm / π ≈ 0.318 Vm

Vavg,full = 2Vm / π ≈ 0.637 Vm

Vm = √2 × VRMS

These are ideal, unfiltered, single-phase sinusoidal results. Diode forward drops, source impedance, load type and capacitor filtering change practical output voltage.

3. Worked examples

Example A: half-wave average

For Vm = 20 V: Vavg = 20/π ≈ 6.37 V (ideal, unfiltered).

Example B: full-wave average

For Vm = 20 V: Vavg = 40/π ≈ 12.73 V (ideal, unfiltered).

Example C: peak voltage

A 12 V RMS sine wave has peak voltage √2 × 12 ≈ 16.97 V.

4. Ripple and capacitor filtering

The full-wave rectified waveform repeats at twice the input frequency; the half-wave waveform repeats at the input frequency. A capacitor across the load charges near peaks and supplies current between peaks, reducing output ripple under suitable operating conditions.

ΔV ≈ Iload / (fripple × C)

This is an approximate peak-to-peak ripple relationship for a capacitor-input filter with roughly constant load current and sufficiently small ripple. It is not a general exact equation.

Example: a 100 Hz ripple frequency, 0.1 A load current and 1000 μF (0.001 F) capacitor give ΔV ≈ 0.1/(100 × 0.001) = 1 V peak-to-peak approximately.

Capacitor voltage rating, ripple-current rating, inrush current and discharge time matter in actual designs.

5. Uncontrolled and controlled rectifiers

Uncontrolled rectifiers use diodes and conduct according to source polarity and circuit conditions. Phase-controlled rectifiers commonly use thyristors (SCRs), whose turn-on can be delayed by a gate trigger to control average output voltage under appropriate circuit conditions.

An SCR typically remains conducting until current falls below its holding-current condition; a gate pulse does not ordinarily turn off a conventional SCR. Controlled rectifiers introduce additional considerations including power factor and harmonics.

6. Safety and exercises

Do not connect experimental rectifiers directly to household mains. Use suitably isolated, low-voltage, current-limited teaching equipment with supervision. Filter capacitors may retain hazardous charge after disconnection; follow verified discharge and measurement procedures.
