Electrical & Electronics Fundamentals

Learn the core electrical quantities and circuit relationships used throughout power electronics. Estimated study time: 35–50 minutes.

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish voltage, current, resistance and power.
  • Apply Ohm’s law and calculate electrical power.
  • Recognize series and parallel connections and basic AC/DC concepts.
  • Identify important components and elementary laboratory safety practices.

1. Essential electrical quantities

Voltage (V)

Potential difference between two points; the driving force for current in a circuit. Unit: volt (V).

Current (I)

Rate of electric charge flow. Unit: ampere (A). Conventional current flows from higher to lower potential in an external circuit.

Resistance (R)

Opposition to current flow. Unit: ohm (Ω). In an ideal resistor, voltage and current obey Ohm’s law.

Power (P)

Rate of electrical energy transfer. Unit: watt (W). For DC circuits, P = VI.

2. Fundamental equations

V = I × R

Ohm’s law for an ohmic resistor. Rearranged: I = V/R and R = V/I.

P = V × I = I²R = V²/R

These power forms apply to a DC resistive load. For AC circuits, real power depends on power factor and waveform.

E = P × t

Energy equals constant power multiplied by time. For example, 100 W used for 2 hours is 200 Wh = 0.2 kWh.

3. Worked examples

Example A: current

A 12 V DC supply is connected across a 6 Ω resistor.

I = V/R = 12/6 = 2 A.

Example B: power

Using the same 12 V supply and 2 A current:

P = VI = 12 × 2 = 24 W.

Example C: series

Two ideal resistors of 4 Ω and 6 Ω in series have an equivalent resistance of 10 Ω.

Example D: parallel

Two ideal 6 Ω resistors in parallel have equivalent resistance of 3 Ω.

4. DC, AC and common components

DC: current maintains one direction, although its magnitude may vary. AC: current reverses direction periodically; mains electricity is commonly AC.

Resistor: limits current or divides voltage. Capacitor: stores energy in an electric field and can smooth voltage. Inductor: stores energy in a magnetic field and opposes rapid changes in current. Diode: ideally conducts in one direction and blocks in the other; real devices have limitations.

Power converters combine switching devices, inductors, capacitors and control circuitry to change voltage or waveform.

5. Safety first

Use only low-voltage, current-limited educational circuits under suitable supervision. Disconnect power before changing connections. Capacitors can retain charge after disconnection. Never experiment directly with mains voltage or energized power-electronic equipment.

6. Practice exercises

  1. Find the current through a 10 Ω resistor connected to 20 V DC.
  2. Find the power used by a DC load operating at 24 V and 3 A.
  3. Calculate the total resistance of 5 Ω and 15 Ω connected in series.
Show answers

1) 2 A. 2) 72 W. 3) 20 Ω.
