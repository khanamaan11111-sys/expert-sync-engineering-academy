DC–DC Converters

Learn the operating principles of buck, boost and buck–boost converters, duty cycle, inductor energy storage and basic efficiency calculations. Estimated study time: 50–65 minutes.

Learning objectives

  • Identify the purpose and core components of switching DC–DC converters.
  • Use ideal continuous-conduction-mode (CCM) voltage conversion ratios.
  • Calculate duty cycle and approximate efficiency.
  • Understand the limitations of ideal models and safe simulation practice.

1. What does a DC–DC converter do?

A DC–DC converter transforms one DC voltage level into another using controlled semiconductor switching and energy-storage elements. A switching converter typically uses a transistor, diode or synchronous switch, inductor, capacitor and control circuit. Its output depends on topology, duty cycle, load and losses.

Buck converter

Step-down topology: ideal output voltage is lower than the input. The inductor supplies current to the load during both switching intervals.

Boost converter

Step-up topology: an inductor stores energy while the switch is on and releases energy through the output path when the switch is off.

Inverting buck–boost

Can step the magnitude of voltage up or down; its output polarity is opposite to the input reference in the classical single-switch topology.

2. Switching duty cycle

Duty cycle D is the fraction of each switching period during which the controlled switch is on:

D = ton / T = ton × fs

For example, if a switch is on for 4 μs in a 10 μs period, D = 0.4 = 40%. Switching frequency fs = 1/T, so the example operates at 100 kHz.

For the ideal relations below, assume steady-state operation, continuous inductor current, ideal components and negligible losses. Real converters need feedback regulation and component ratings appropriate to their operating conditions.

3. Ideal voltage conversion ratios

Buck: Vout = D × Vin

Boost: Vout = Vin / (1 − D)

Inverting buck–boost: Vout = −[D / (1 − D)] × Vin

These ideal CCM expressions do not apply universally in discontinuous-conduction mode (DCM), startup, current limit or nonideal operating conditions. In a boost converter, D must be less than 1; practical output is limited by losses and component ratings.

4. Worked examples

Example A: buck

Given Vin = 24 V and D = 0.5: ideal Vout = 0.5 × 24 = 12 V.

Example B: boost

Given Vin = 12 V and D = 0.4: ideal Vout = 12/(1 − 0.4) = 20 V.

Example C: inverting buck–boost

Given Vin = 10 V and D = 0.6: ideal Vout = −10 × 0.6/0.4 = −15 V.

5. Inductor, capacitor and efficiency

An inductor resists abrupt current changes and temporarily stores energy in its magnetic field. An output capacitor helps smooth voltage ripple. Their selection affects ripple, transient response, size and current/voltage stress.

η = (Pout / Pin) × 100%

Example: if measured input power is 50 W and output power is 45 W, efficiency = 45/50 × 100 = 90%. Losses may include semiconductor conduction and switching losses, magnetic losses, capacitor losses and control power.

In a real converter, a higher duty cycle does not guarantee unlimited voltage gain. Consider saturation, switching stress, thermal limits, minimum on/off times and control-loop stability.

6. Simulation exercise

In a suitable circuit simulator, create an idealized, low-voltage buck converter with 24 V input, a 40% switch duty cycle, an inductor, output capacitor and resistive load. Compare the steady-state average output with the ideal target of 9.6 V. Observe switch-node voltage, inductor current and output ripple. Then vary duty cycle and record the result.

Simulation output depends on component values, solver settings, model assumptions and whether the circuit has reached steady state. The ideal ratio is a reference, not a guarantee for every model.

7. Safety and practice

Start with simulation and suitably supervised low-voltage, current-limited educational setups. Never connect experimental converter circuits directly to mains or high-energy battery packs. Capacitors can remain charged after power is removed; use appropriate isolation, measurement and discharge procedures.
